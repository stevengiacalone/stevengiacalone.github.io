Research

I study how planets and brown dwarfs form and evolve, using ground-based telescopes and large surveys from space missions like TESS. Four threads run through my work.

01

{{ th[0].title }}

{{ th[0].role }}

Brown dwarfs, objects traditionally classified as having 13–80 times the mass of Jupiter, are thought to form far from their host stars or free-floating. Brown dwarfs orbiting close to their stars have therefore been a mystery since their discovery decades ago. Did these massive companions migrate inward from distant orbits, or did they form in situ through some unknown mechanism? To answer this, I use ground-based observations that reveal the orbits, host-star properties, and atmospheric compositions of brown dwarfs.

Orbital tilts

The angle between the host star's spin axis and the companion's orbital angular momentum, called the stellar obliquity, records the system's dynamical history. If the two are aligned, the brown dwarf most likely formed in and migrated through a protoplanetary disk, like a planet. If they are misaligned, it was most likely "kicked" inward by another object or captured by the star. To find out how close-in brown dwarfs reached their current orbits, I co-founded the OATMEAL survey, which is more than doubling the number of these systems with obliquity measurements and enabling population-level studies of brown dwarf orbits. See Giacalone et al. (2024) and Giacalone et al. (2025).

Stellar metallicities

Stellar metallicity correlates strongly with how often giant planets and low-mass stellar companions occur. Giant planets are more common around metal-rich stars, whereas low-mass stellar companions appear around stars spanning a wide range of metallicities. This is likely because giant planet formation (gas accreting onto a solid core) works more efficiently in metal-rich environments, while star formation (direct collapse of gas) is largely independent of metal content. In principle, we can tell whether brown dwarfs form like planets or like stars by finding the companion mass at which the host-star metallicity distribution changes. In a submitted paper, we explore this using the California Legacy Survey, a 30-year radial-velocity survey that detected hundreds of planets and brown dwarfs.

Atmospheric compositions

I am leading a survey to measure the atmospheric compositions of close-in brown dwarfs with high-resolution, near-infrared spectroscopy. The goal is to measure their carbon-to-oxygen ratios, which trace how and where they formed. Comparing these with giant-planet measurements will show whether brown dwarfs and giant planets share a formation pathway.

For my PhD thesis, I measured how common small planets are close to A-type stars for the first time. A-type stars are hot stars roughly twice the size of the Sun. Those properties may give their planets different characteristics from planets around Sun-like stars, but they also make planets around them harder to find, so early surveys such as Kepler largely avoided them. Because TESS observes stars across nearly the entire sky, it finally lets us study planets around A-type stars. In Giacalone & Dressing (2025), I found that planets smaller than Jupiter with orbital periods under 10 days are significantly rarer around A-type stars than around cooler stars. This is likely because dust sublimates farther from these hot stars, which keeps small planets from forming in or migrating to such close orbits.

When TESS launched in 2018, it was expected to find thousands of transiting planets and thousands of astrophysical false positives, such as eclipsing binaries that mimic planetary signals. Radial-velocity measurements or transit-timing variations can confirm genuine planets, but limited telescope time makes that impossible for most candidates. To address this bottleneck, I developed TRICERATOPS, a Bayesian tool that distinguishes true planets from false positives using TESS photometry alone (Giacalone et al. 2021).

With TRICERATOPS, I discovered 13 rocky planets that are high-priority targets for JWST atmospheric characterization (Giacalone et al. 2022a) and the Neptune-size planet HD 56414 b, one of the only known sub-Jovian planets orbiting an A-type star (Giacalone et al. 2022b). The tool is now responsible for more than 100 planet discoveries, roughly 20% of all planets found in TESS data to date, and is widely used across the exoplanet community.

Comparing observations with theoretical models tells us how planets form and evolve. In Giacalone et al. (2017) and Königl et al. (2017), I compared simulations of high-eccentricity tidal migration, a way of moving planets from wide orbits to close-in ones, with the observed population of close-in planets. We showed that this mechanism reproduces the eccentricity distribution of hot Jupiters and the orbital-period distribution of dynamically isolated rocky planets, explaining how planets end up where they could not have formed.

In Giacalone et al. (2019), I simulated dust carried radially outward through protoplanetary disks by magnetocentrifugal winds. Outward transport is thought to occur because of calcium-aluminum-rich inclusions in carbonaceous chondrite meteorites and the large amounts of crystalline dust in the outer regions of protoplanetary disks, both of which could only have formed at high temperatures close to the star. I showed that disk-wind transport can explain the observed crystallinity fractions in outer disks, suggesting it plays an important role in planet formation.
