NASA's Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS), launched in 2018, finds planets by watching for the tiny dip in a star's brightness when a planet passes in front of it. The catch is that many other things can cause the same dip, such as two stars eclipsing each other, or a faint background star whose light is blended in with the target. Confirming each candidate with large telescopes is slow and expensive, and TESS produces thousands of them.

TRICERATOPS: telling real planets from impostors

To clear that bottleneck I built TRICERATOPS, a statistical tool that weighs every plausible explanation for a signal, including nearby stars that could be the real source, and calculates the probability that it comes from a genuine planet (Giacalone et al. 2021). This process is referred to as "statistical validation."

What it has found

TRICERATOPS is now widely used across the exoplanet community and credited with more than 200 planet discoveries, a number that continues to grow every day. During my PhD, I used my tool to statistically validate 13 hot, potentially rocky planets that are prime targets for the James Webb Space Telescope to study the surfaces and atmospheres of rocky worlds (Giacalone et al. 2022a). Many of these planets are now active targets of JWST programs. I also used it to statistically validate HD 56414 b, a rare Neptune-size planet orbiting a young A-type star. It is one of only four known planets smaller than Jupiter around an early-type star (Giacalone et al. 2022b).
