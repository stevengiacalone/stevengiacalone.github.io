NASA's Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS), launched in 2018, finds planets by watching for the tiny dip in a star's brightness when a planet passes in front of it. The catch is that many other things can cause the same dip, such as two stars eclipsing each other, or a faint background star whose light is blended in with the target. Confirming each candidate with large telescopes is slow and expensive, and TESS produces thousands of them.

TRICERATOPS: telling real planets from impostors

To clear that bottleneck I built TRICERATOPS, a statistical tool that weighs every plausible explanation for a signal, including nearby stars that could be the real source, and calculates the probability that it comes from a genuine planet. It needs only TESS data and information about the surrounding stars, and when it was released it was the only TESS tool that accounted for false alarms from neighboring stars (Giacalone et al. 2021). TRICERATOPS is now credited with more than 200 planet discoveries and is widely used across the exoplanet community.

What it has found

Among its discoveries are 13 hot, potentially rocky planets that are prime targets for the James Webb Space Telescope to study the surfaces and atmospheres of rocky worlds (Giacalone et al. 2022a). Another is HD 56414 b, a Neptune-size planet orbiting a young A-type star, one of the hottest stars known to host a planet smaller than Jupiter. It suggests that the "hot Neptune desert," a striking shortage of Neptune-size planets very close to their stars, extends to hot stars too (Giacalone et al. 2022b).
