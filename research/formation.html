Many planets live where they could not have been born. Hot Jupiters, for example, orbit far closer to their stars than Mercury does to the Sun, where it is too hot for a gas giant to form. Simulations let us test how planets, and the material that builds them, end up so far from where they started.

Planets on the move

One explanation is high-eccentricity migration. A planet is knocked onto a long, stretched orbit that swings very close to its star. At each close pass, tides sap a little of its energy until the orbit shrinks into a tight circle. By comparing simulations of this process with the planets we actually observe, we showed that it reproduces the orbital shapes of hot Jupiters (Giacalone et al. 2017). It may also explain a group of lonely, Earth-size planets that orbit their stars in about a day: they could be the rocky cores of giant planets that strayed too close and had their gas stripped away (Königl et al. 2017).

Dust that traveled outward

Some of the dust in planet-forming disks, and in certain meteorites that fall to Earth, is crystalline, which means it was baked at high temperatures close to the young star. Yet this dust turns up in the cold outer reaches of disks, far from where it was made. I simulated how magnetically driven winds can lift dust grains off the inner disk and drop them much farther out. The model reproduces the amount of crystalline dust seen in the outer parts of real disks, suggesting these winds help shape the raw material of planets (Giacalone et al. 2019).
