Comparing observations with theoretical models tells us how planets form and evolve. In Giacalone et al. (2017) and Königl et al. (2017), I compared simulations of high-eccentricity tidal migration, a way of moving planets from wide orbits to close-in ones, with the observed population of close-in planets. We showed that this mechanism reproduces the eccentricity distribution of hot Jupiters and the orbital-period distribution of dynamically isolated rocky planets, explaining how planets end up where they could not have formed.

In Giacalone et al. (2019), I simulated dust carried radially outward through protoplanetary disks by magnetocentrifugal winds. Outward transport is thought to occur because of calcium-aluminum-rich inclusions in carbonaceous chondrite meteorites and the large amounts of crystalline dust in the outer regions of protoplanetary disks, both of which could only have formed at high temperatures close to the star. I showed that disk-wind transport can explain the observed crystallinity fractions in outer disks, suggesting it plays an important role in planet formation.

Brown dwarf atmospheres

I am leading a survey to measure the atmospheric compositions of close-in brown dwarfs with high-resolution, near-infrared spectroscopy. The goal is to measure their carbon-to-oxygen ratios, which trace how and where they formed. Comparing these with giant-planet measurements will show whether brown dwarfs and giant planets share a formation pathway.
