How common planets are, and how that depends on the stars they orbit, holds clues to how they form. I use large, homogeneous surveys to measure these trends across whole populations.

Giant planets versus brown dwarfs

Stellar metallicity correlates strongly with how often giant planets and low-mass stellar companions occur. Giant planets are more common around metal-rich stars, whereas low-mass stellar companions appear around stars spanning a wide range of metallicities. This is likely because giant planet formation (gas accreting onto a solid core) works more efficiently in metal-rich environments, while star formation (direct collapse of gas) is largely independent of metal content. In principle, we can tell whether brown dwarfs form like planets or like stars by finding the companion mass at which the host-star metallicity distribution changes. In Giacalone et al. (2026), we explore this using the California Legacy Survey, a 30-year radial-velocity survey that detected hundreds of planets and brown dwarfs.

Small planets around hot stars

For my PhD thesis, I measured how common small planets are close to A-type stars for the first time. A-type stars are hot stars roughly twice the size of the Sun. Those properties may give their planets different characteristics from planets around Sun-like stars, but they also make planets around them harder to find, so early surveys such as Kepler largely avoided them. Because TESS observes stars across nearly the entire sky, it finally lets us study planets around A-type stars. In Giacalone & Dressing (2025), I found that planets smaller than Jupiter with orbital periods under 10 days are significantly rarer around A-type stars than around cooler stars. This is likely because dust sublimates farther from these hot stars, which keeps small planets from forming in or migrating to such close orbits.
