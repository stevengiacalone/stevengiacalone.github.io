The angle between a star's spin and its companion's orbit, called the stellar obliquity, is a fossil record of the companion's past. In our own solar system, the planets orbit within about 7 degrees of the Sun's equator, a sign that they formed together in the flat disk of gas and dust that once surrounded the young Sun. A companion on a similarly aligned orbit most likely formed the same way and drifted inward gently through that disk. A polar orbit, tilted by about 90 degrees, points to a more turbulent history, such as gravitational tugs from a distant star or planet that twisted the orbit and drove the companion inward. A retrograde orbit, near 180 degrees, means the companion travels in the opposite direction to the star's spin, one of the clearest signs of a dramatic past.

Where did close-in brown dwarfs come from?

Brown dwarfs, objects many tens of times more massive than Jupiter, are too heavy to be ordinary planets but too light to shine like stars. They are thought to form far from their stars, so the ones found on tight orbits have been a puzzle for decades. To work out how they got there, I co-founded the OATMEAL survey, which aims to triple the number of these systems with a measured stellar obliquity. A priori, one might expect the stellar obliquity distribution for brown dwarfs to resemble that of hot Jupiters. Our first finding was therefore a surprise: the brown dwarf GPX-1 b orbits neatly along its star's equator, even though hot Jupiters around similar stars are often badly misaligned (Giacalone et al. 2024).

Follow-up OATMEAL papers hint at a broader trend for spin-orbit alignment among close-in brown dwarfs, even when their orbits are wide and eccentric (Carmichael et al. 2026, Vowell et al. 2026), suggesting that these objects migrate inwards in the plane of the protoplanetary disk. Further investigation is needed to determine if this trend holds across a larger sample.

A planet going the wrong way

Hot Jupiters are giant planets that orbit their stars in just a few days. Around Sun-like stars, tides are expected to pull their orbits back into line, so they are usually aligned. KELT-23A b breaks that rule: it appears to orbit backward relative to its star's spin, and its true path may run closer to over the star's poles. That suggests it arrived recently, possibly nudged by a companion star 570 times farther out than the Earth is from the Sun. A simple model built from this system shows why hot Jupiters in wide binary-star systems tend to end up either aligned or on polar orbits (Giacalone et al. 2025).
