The angle between a star's spin axis and the orbital axis of its companion, called the stellar obliquity, records the system's dynamical history. If the two are aligned, the companion most likely formed in and migrated through a protoplanetary disk, like a planet. If they are misaligned, it was most likely "kicked" inward by another object or captured by the star.

Brown dwarfs, objects traditionally classified as having 13–80 times the mass of Jupiter, are thought to form far from their host stars or free-floating, so those on close orbits have been a mystery since their discovery decades ago. To find out how they reached their current orbits, I co-founded the OATMEAL survey, which is more than doubling the number of these systems with obliquity measurements and enabling population-level studies of brown dwarf orbits (Giacalone et al. 2024). I also measure obliquities for hot Jupiters to compare the two populations, including a hot Jupiter on a retrograde orbit around a Sun-like star (Giacalone et al. 2025).
